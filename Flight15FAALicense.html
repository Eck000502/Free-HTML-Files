Status: license not issued As of Friday morning, Nov 13, 2026 (CT) Window: 4:45–6:00 PM CT · Pad 2 · Booster 22 + Ship 42

Why the Flight 15 FAA license isn't ready yet

The honest answer, after checking the FAA's own record, SpaceX's statements, and the credible press: nobody in authority has given a reason — the FAA didn't even answer Reuters' request for comment. What the record does show is what is unsigned, why it's a first-of-its-kind safety case, and how late "late" really is against this program's own history.

The bottom line

Flight 15 cannot fly on the existing license at all — and the modification it needs is the first safety case of its kind the FAA has ever had to sign.

Flight 14's license (VOL 23-129, Revision 9.0) covered that flight alone. Flight 15 needs a new modification, and its novel element isn't the launch or the booster catch — both have been licensed before. It's authorizing the Ship to reenter over land, across northern Mexico and southern Texas and over populated areas, and be caught at Pad 2. Every prior Ship reentry was over open ocean, by design.

The environmental groundwork for that corridor was finished months ago (final tiered EA/FONSI signed Sept. 2, 2026), and SpaceX says the conditions — the go/no-go rulebook a returning ship must meet — are already FAA-approved. What's missing is the Part 450 safety and policy sign-off embodied in the license document itself. In this program that signature habitually arrives 1–2 days before flight. On Friday morning it hadn't — roughly a day late against the pattern, with backup windows running Nov. 14–20.

What remains genuinely unknown: whether the FAA's safety review is stuck on any particular element, whether SpaceX was asked for anything more, and whether the license — if it slips — would arrive whole or stripped of the ship catch. No source answers those questions, because no official has addressed them.

Question 1 · Has anyone stated a reason?

No one in authority has given a reason

This is the single best-established fact in the whole story. The FAA's position, in full, is silence.

"SpaceX as of Friday morning had not received a launch license from the US Federal Aviation Administration. The FAA, which has typically issued licenses a day prior to past Starship launches, did not immediately respond to a request for comment." Reuters, Friday Nov 13, 2026 b — reporting the FAA's non-response a

SpaceX's only on-record words about the license are on the Flight 15 mission page, verified live on Friday morning:

"Flight 15 of Starship is preparing to launch as soon as Friday, November 13, pending regulatory approval. The 75-minute launch window will open at 4:45 p.m. CT… the schedule is dynamic and likely to change." SpaceX, Flight 15 mission page a

SpaceX's most substantive regulatory statement predates the delay and is forward-looking, not an explanation. President Gwynne Shotwell, on the Oct. 20 Q3 earnings call:

"We have had significant constructive engagement with the FAA, and we expect the license for our Flight 15 to include return to launch site and tower catch for both stages." Gwynne Shotwell, Oct 20, 2026, as quoted in coverage of the call a

SpaceX's Nov. 7 safety update, "Returning Starship to the Launch Site," describes the return profile in detail and says the return conditions are FAA-approved — and contains no complaint, estimate, or explanation about license timing at all. a NASASpaceFlight's Nov. 13 flight article says only that "the FAA has not yet published an updated launch license for the flight," and Teslarati reduces it to one line: SpaceX was "still waiting on the FAA to approve a license change covering the ship's return over land." b The New York Times' explainer likewise notes only that the FAA "had not yet issued a license" as of Friday morning. b None offers a cause, because none was given.

One thing that is not holding Flight 15: a mishap investigation. After Flight 14, an FAA spokesperson told Ars Technica that "all flight events for both the Starship vehicle and the Super Heavy booster occurred within the scope of planned and FAA authorized activities" — so no mishap process stands between Flight 14 and Flight 15. a

Question 2 · What specifically remains to be approved?

The unsigned piece: a first-ever overland reentry

Starship flies under FAA vehicle operator license VOL 23-129. Flight 14 flew under Revision 9.0 (issued and effective Sept. 26, 2026), structured as Order A-1 for launch and Order A-2 for reentry — and it covered Flight 14 alone. Flight 15 therefore needs a further modification before any version of the flight can launch. b

b

The modification must newly authorize the ship's return to the tower

"SpaceX now needs FAA approval of a licence modification for Flight 15, including permission to bring Ship 42 back to the tower at Starbase, which will involve an overflight of populated areas during the return. Until that is in hand, the dual-catch profile cannot fly as planned." The booster catch, by contrast, has been licensed before, and the launch/orbital/Starlink profile largely repeats Flight 14. Source: NASASpaceFlight, Nov 6, 2026

a

The corridor: Pacific reentry, over Mexico and Texas, weaving around the Valley's cities

Per SpaceX's Nov. 7 update: after a single-Raptor deorbit burn, reentry begins over the Pacific "thousands of kilometers uprange from Starbase"; the ship uses its four flaps to "intentionally fly over sparsely populated areas of northern Mexico and southern Texas," then "weave its way around the major cities in the Rio Grande Valley, passing to the north of South Padre Island and out over the Gulf of America" before the final turn to Pad 2. Built-in safeguards the FAA is licensing: a passively safe suborbital ascent (default outcome is the Indian Ocean), three in-flight disposal gates (north of Hawaii ~T+1:28, off Chile ~T+7:16, North Pacific ~T+20:42), an in-entry option to tumble the ship so it breaks up and burns up before reaching land, and a late divert into the Gulf if ship or tower is not healthy. Source: SpaceX, "Returning Starship to the Launch Site," Nov 7, 2026

a

The safety math the FAA must sign: Ec ≤ 1×10⁻⁴, Pc ≤ 1×10⁻⁶

The FAA's own Starship page states its license evaluation covers "1) public safety issues (such as overflight of populated areas and payload contents); 2) national security or foreign policy concerns; 3) insurance requirements for the launch operator; and 4) potential environmental impact." Under 14 CFR Part 450 (§450.101), which governs VOL 23-129, the binding tests for reentry — measured "from the final health check prior to initiating deorbit through final impact or landing" — are a collective risk to the public no greater than an expected number of casualties Ec ≤ 1×10⁻⁴, and individual risk Pc ≤ 1×10⁻⁶, counting impacting debris, toxic release, and far-field blast overpressure. Aircraft risk (Pc ≤ 1×10⁻⁶) is what generates the hazard areas and NOTAMs. No source publishes Flight 15's actual computed Ec, and no source claims it fails or is disputed. Sources: FAA, SpaceX Starship Super Heavy Project at Boca Chica · 14 CFR 450.101

a

"Conditions are FAA-approved" — the rulebook is signed, the license isn't

SpaceX's update says flight controllers "will only permit Starship to return to Starbase if the vehicle's status meets our internally developed, and FAA-approved, conditions for a return to launch site and catch" — coast-phase health checks on Raptor health, power and comms, flight computers and navigation, tank and feedline structure, tank pressure, and attitude control, plus heat-shield imaging from three camera-equipped Starlinks. That approves the in-flight decision rulebook. It is not the launch license — a distinction multiple trackers have stressed. Source: SpaceX, Nov 7, 2026 update

a

Environmental review is already complete — it is not the open item

The FAA's Starship page (last updated Nov. 10, 2026) lists as complete both the Final Tiered EA/FONSI for return-to-launch-site mission profiles and airspace closures, and the Final Tiered EA/FONSI/ROD for reentry contingency operations and the additional landing trajectory to Boca Chica — signed Sept. 2, 2026, with the Federal Register notice on Sept. 4. That notice itself stresses SpaceX "must obtain a modification to their existing vehicle operator license" — environmental clearance is not a license, a distinction FAA Public Affairs also made explicitly in September. The separate environmental impact statement for 100–456 launches per year is a future-cadence review and is not a Flight 15 gate. Sonic boom was an item in those completed reviews; SpaceX notes Cameron County residents "may hear one or more sonic booms" and that the boom "does not present a health risk." No source reports a boom-specific hold-up. Sources: FAA Starship project page · Federal Register notice, Sept 4, 2026

a

Interagency consultations are required by rule — none is reported outstanding

Part 450's policy review (§450.41) requires the FAA to consult the Department of Defense (national security) and the Department of State (foreign policy and international obligations — the channel through which a routine overflight of Mexico would be addressed), plus NASA and other agencies as needed; payload review (§450.43, for the 26 Starlink V3 satellites) has parallel consultations. Endangered Species Act §7 consultation with the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service and NOAA Fisheries attaches to the environmental review — completed for this profile — and was the documented pacing item for the first booster catch in 2024, not reported as open now. Airspace coordination is visibly done: the FAA Operations Advisory lists launch and reentry windows, and NOTAMs are filed for the Mexico ACC corridor, Mexico FIR, Piarco FIR, Oakland Oceanic FIR contingency closures, and the Indian Ocean disposal area. No source reports any of these consultations as incomplete or contested for Flight 15 — their internal status is simply not public. Sources: 14 CFR Part 450 (§§450.41, 450.43) · NOTAMs as compiled by trackers b/c

Question 3 · What do credible reporters say is the sticking point?

The ship-catch safety case is the pacing item — stated as novelty, not dispute

The consensus across the credible press is consistent, and notably free of conflict. NASASpaceFlight's framing from late October through Nov. 13: the date "slipped by only one day, with the ship-catch licence from the FAA still the deciding factor"; "regulatory steps, above all the ship-catch case, could still move that target to the right"; "for now, it all depends on one item: the licence change that lets Ship 42 come home to the tower." b Ars Technica's contribution to the record is reporting, after Flight 14, that a ship catch "will require reentry over land, necessitating additional safety reviews to ensure no undue risk to populated areas" — the overland public-safety review is the known work item. b

The explicit negative finding matters as much: across Reuters, NASASpaceFlight (two articles), Teslarati, the New York Times, USA Today, Space.com-derived and local Texas coverage reviewed, there is no report of a specific technical or safety dispute, an FAA request for additional data from SpaceX, a requested change to the flight profile (such as splashdown instead of catch), or any named official commenting on the delay at all. b SpaceNews and Florida Today coverage found adds nothing on cause.

Question 4 · Is a launch-day license precedented?

How late is late? The program's own pattern

Last-minute issuance for a first-of-its-kind catch is the historical norm for this program. Flight 15 is tighter than any precedent found — but a slip into the backup window would be entirely in-pattern.

FlightMilestoneLicense issuedGap before launch
Flight 1 First integrated flight · Apr 20, 2023 VOL 23-129 originally issued Apr 14, 2023 a 6 days
Flight 5 First booster catch · Oct 13, 2024 Modification issued Sat Oct 12, 2024 — FAA: SpaceX "met all safety, environmental and other licensing requirements" ab 1 day
Flight 14 First orbital flight · Sep 28, 2026 Rev. 9.0 issued and effective Sep 26, 2026 a 2 days
Flight 15 First ship catch / dual catch · target Nov 13, 2026 Not issued as of Friday morning b — pending

The closest precedent cuts both ways, and it's Flight 5. For the first booster catch, the FAA had publicly signaled a decision not until late November, driven by 60-day Endangered Species Act consultations with Fish & Wildlife (sonic boom over land) and NOAA Fisheries (a changed hot-stage splashdown) — and then issued the license the day before launch anyway, ahead of its own signaled timeline. Agency-signaled timelines have proven pessimistic before. b

Verdict: a license arriving Friday afternoon, on launch day, would be tighter than any precedent found (the tightest is Flight 5's one day). On Reuters' "typically a day prior" pattern, a Friday-morning absence is roughly a day late — noticeable, not yet extraordinary.

The "FAA said late November" story is about Flight 5 — in 2024

Verified false for Flight 15 do not use

"SpaceX recently received a launch license date estimate of late November from the FAA… not based on a new safety concern, but instead driven by superfluous environmental analysis," with "four open environmental issues."

Two low-tier newsletters (Stellar Times, Nov. 9; Paulli's Nyhedsbrief, Nov. 9) attribute that language to SpaceX's Nov. 7, 2026 update. The complete text of the Nov. 7, 2026 update was read and verified: it contains no such statement, no license-timing estimate, and no environmental complaint. Question 5 · Could it already be issued but not yet public?

Possible in principle — but three signals all read "not issued"

In this program, issuance has historically become public within hours. As of Friday morning, every independent public signal pointed the same way:

  • FAA license documents (Document Retrieval System / commercial-space listings) Not published — NSF, Nov 13: "the FAA has not yet published an updated launch license"
    Precedent: Rev. 9.0 for Flight 14, dated Sept. 26, was screenshotted from the FAA document system and posted the same day, showing the license number, revision, effective date, and the signature of Ansel L. Collins, Manager, Safety Authorization Division. A Rev. 10 would surface the same way. b
  • SpaceX's mission page wording Still reads "pending regulatory approval" (verified live Friday morning)
    SpaceX historically updates the page and posts the firm target once licensed — as with Flight 5, when it posted the Sunday target immediately upon the Oct. 12 issuance. a
  • NOTAMs, TFRs, and the FAA Operations Advisory Fully in place — but they are not evidence of a license
    The Ops Advisory lists the Nov. 13 launch window (4:45–6:40 PM CT) and Nov. 14 reentry window (2:56–6:14 PM CT) with backups; TFRs over Brownsville are active; Mexico, Oakland Oceanic, Piarco, and Indian Ocean NOTAMs are filed. These are planning instruments issued ahead of a license. A change in this set would signal a slip, not an issuance. As the Vermilion Times put it Friday morning: the FAA air-traffic plan lists the flight, but "the Times could not find a published FAA license for this flight, and SpaceX still lists it as pending regulatory approval." b

Note: the FAA's Starship project page is an environmental-documents page; its silence is not itself the license signal. The document listing and SpaceX's page are.

Question 6 · Administrative or political factors?

No evidence links politics or staffing to this license

None — in any source reviewed. No Flight 15 article from Reuters, NASASpaceFlight, Teslarati, or the New York Times mentions the government shutdown, staffing, or politics in connection with the license, and no reporting was found on staffing levels or workload inside the FAA's Office of Commercial Space Transportation (the office that issues VOL 23-129) in November 2026. The general controller-shortage reporting concerns air traffic operations, not that office. b

The administration's posture on the record points the other way: on Nov. 5, 2026, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy announced four FAA actions to streamline commercial space launch and reentry licensing, with a broader overhaul planned for 2027 and expanded hiring in the licensing office — eight days before Flight 15. No statement by the administration, Elon Musk, or SpaceX criticizing or pressuring the FAA over Flight 15 was found. Contrast 2024, when SpaceX publicly attacked the Flight 5 timeline: the absence of any such campaign in 2026 is itself weak evidence SpaceX does not regard the timing as aberrant. a b

c

The shutdown theory: plausible-sounding, unverified, date-contaminated

Secondary sources dated 2026 describe a federal shutdown beginning Oct. 1, lasting 43 days and ending Nov. 12, with the FAA ordering flight cuts at 40 airports from Nov. 7 and an emergency order restricting commercial space launches and reentries to 10 PM–6 AM local time. If that timeline is accurate, licensing staff would have returned to work the day before launch day — a plausible drag on any pending review. But: no Flight 15 source mentions the shutdown in connection with the license; key underlying items in the search index are dated November 2025 (the quoted curfew order is expressly "beginning at 6:00 a.m. EST on November 10, 2025," and weekday details in "2026" retellings don't fit the 2026 calendar), indicating the index conflates the Nov. 2025 shutdown with 2026-dated rewrites; and a 4:45 PM CT launch is itself inconsistent with a still-active overnight-only spaceflight order, with no source reporting an exemption or conflict. Conclusion: cannot be confirmed as a factor — an open question, not an explanation. Sources: Air1 · Daily Pulse USA — both flagged unreliable as applied here
